My research focuses on children's moral behavior, such as cheating and lying. Particularly, I examine the role of the self in moral behavior across two main lines of work.

## First: Children's negative self-attitudes and moral development

I find that children's dishonest behavior is not only related to cognitive, neural ([Yu, Dai et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dcn.2025.101613)) and environmental factors, but also to how they perceive themselves. Children who exhibit higher self-criticism ([Yu, Tay et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1093/chidev/aacaf049)) and more dysfunctional self-related attitudes ([Yu, Ding, & Hong, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0002117)) are more likely to engage in dishonest behavior. In these cases, dishonesty may function as a compensatory strategy to obtain external validation or as a protective strategy to avoid disapproval. In contrast, children in more supportive environments that enhance self-esteem tend to show higher levels of honesty (Yu, Zhang et al., in prep).

## Second: Children's moral identity

I explore how young children construct their moral identity (i.e., the ideal self they aspire to in a moral sense; Hardy et al., 2014) and how this moral identity operates in early development. Our studies suggest that after moral transgressions, children already show a motivation to become a "better" person (Yu, Chun et al., in prep). Unlike adults, they are less likely to defensively justify their transgression; instead, they evaluate the transgression more harshly after they themselves transgress (Yu, Chua et al., in prep).

## Broader interests

I am also interested in supporting early education, including how to foster children's moral learning ([Foo et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0002005)) and science learning ([Yu et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2025.102187)).
